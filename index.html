From Floral Waste to a Greener Future

Pushpa BioPlastic

Pushpa BioPlastic is an eco-friendly biomaterial concept developed using recycled temple flowers and floral waste. By transforming discarded flowers into value-added biodegradable materials, we reduce conventional plastic dependency and promote a circular bioeconomy.

Pack Sizes: 1 kg — Home gardening & balcony pots

02 / Laboratory Process & Benchmarks

Preparation & Testing Studio

Lab Result: Glossy amber-orange floral bioplastic films with embedded petal fibers cast on parchment paper alongside analytical balance & grinder.

01

Sacred Floral Waste Collection & Drying

Sort discarded marigold and rose petals from temples, wash them thoroughly, and shade-dry them to preserve natural pigments and fiber.

02

Feedstock Grinding & Starch Blending

Pulverize the dried petals and combine them with renewable starch, cellulose, sugarcane residue, or algae binders (agar agar — vegetarian gelatin substitute).

03

Plasticizing & Thermal Gelatinization

Add water and 100% pure unscented vegetable glycerine under controlled heating to form a uniform, moldable biopolymer gel; add vanilla culinary essence and orange liquid food colour at this stage.

04

Casting Films & Molding Cups, Bowls & Packaging

Cast the gel as a film on parchment paper to yield a glossy, amber-orange bioplastic film with embedded petal fibers, then press-mold and cure into cups, bowls, cutlery, and eco-friendly packaging.

Materials Used & Formulation Check

Marigold & Rose Petals Agar Agar (Algae Binder) Gelatin (Binder*) 100% Pure Vegetable Glycerine Water Vanilla Culinary Essence Orange Liquid Food Colour

*Presentation checks: Conventional gelatin is animal-derived, which conflicts with vegetarian & temple-sourced messaging — prefer pure agar agar for vegetarian batches. Edible & food-contact safety claims require food-grade inputs and microbial lab testing before certification.

Preparation photo: Dried floral waste, carbonization crucible, and finished Pushpa Biochar granules.

01

Floral Sorting & Moisture Reduction

Separating non-organic offerings and drying temple flowers so pyrolysis runs cleanly and consistently.

02

Controlled Carbonization

Heating floral biomass in a low-oxygen chamber to lock carbon into a porous, stable charcoal matrix.

03

Cooling, Sieving & Quality Checks

Documenting pH, electrical conductivity (EC), carbon content, and nutrient profile prior to horticultural use.

04

Packaging for Greener Spaces

Packed in 1 kg (home gardens), 5 kg (regular gardeners), and 25 kg (nurseries, landscaping & organic farms).

Step 01 of 06

Collection, Washing & Natural Fibre Extraction

Collect banana stems, Colocasia plant material, Calotropis fibres, and discarded flowers from temples, gardens, and flower markets, then wash and extract clean reinforcing fibres.

  • Select healthy plant material; avoid contaminated or rotten waste.
  • Wash thoroughly with clean water to remove dust, mud, and unwanted particles.
  • Separate flowers from leaves and drain excess water.
  • Cut banana stems into pieces; extract fibres by scraping or a fibre-extraction machine, re-wash, and remove moisture for pipe reinforcement.

Visual stage: Cut banana pseudostem sections (a) and washed, extracted plant fibres (b–e) ready for drying.

Short Presentation Explanation

“First, we collect ornamental plant fibres and waste flowers. Then we clean and dry the materials. After drying, we extract and cut the natural fibres into small pieces. We prepare a natural starch binder using corn starch and water. Next, we mix the fibres and flower powder with the binder. We place this mixture into a cylindrical mould and apply pressure to form the pipe. Finally, we dry the pipe, remove it from the mould and finish the surface. In this way, we prepare an eco-friendly BioPipe using natural waste materials.”

PVC vs Bioplastic Pipes

Metric PVC Pipes Bioplastic Pipes
Feedstock Petroleum monomers Starch, cellulose, sugarcane, algae
Tensile strength (literature) 40–50 MPa 3–15 MPa (starch-based bioplastic)
Water absorption (literature) < 0.5 % 20–60 % after 24 h immersion (uncoated starch-based)
Pressure rating (literature) 6–16 bar Target 0.5–2 bar for low-pressure irrigation
End of Life Centuries; microplastics 30–90 % mass loss in 30–90 days (soil burial, literature)
Challenges Fossil carbon footprint Moisture sensitivity, higher cost, standards

03 / Context & Future Roadmap

From Plastic Pollution to Field Applications

Why replacing petroleum conduits matters today — and how natural fiber reinforcement and water-resistant coatings unlock industrial scale.

The Problem with PVC, HDPE & PP

  • Petroleum-based feedstocks tied to fossil extraction
  • Takes hundreds of years to break down in soil
  • Burning releases toxic fumes; weathering creates microplastics
  • Soiled agricultural drip pipes are rarely recycled

Applications & Future Scope

  • Uses: Drip irrigation, drainage, temporary lines & cable conduits
  • Fiber Blends: Natural fibers for higher tensile strength
  • Coatings: Bio-wax barriers to reduce moisture uptake
  • Scale: Industrial extrusion & material certification
